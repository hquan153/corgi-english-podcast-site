Scared to Speak English? Start Small Today! | Corgi English Podcast

Are you scared to speak English because you worry about making mistakes? Learn how small daily speaking habits can help you feel calm and ready.

After this episode, you can use simple daily habits and polite phrases to start speaking English with less fear.

Finding Your Voice Slowly

Emma: Hello, and welcome to Corgi English Podcast. We are so glad you are here with us today. This show is for beginner and intermediate learners who want calm, friendly English practice. Have you ever understood a question in English, but when you opened your mouth, no words came out? That feeling is very common, and we are going to talk about it today. After this episode, you can use simple daily habits and polite phrases to start speaking English with less fear. You do not need to be perfect to begin. My co-host Ryan is here with me to share ideas and examples. Ryan, how are you feeling about this topic today?

Ryan: Hello everyone, and hello Emma. I am really glad we are talking about this. Speaking a new language can feel scary, even when you already know many words. You might worry about sounding silly or making an error in front of others. That fear matters because it can stop you from connecting with people at work or in your neighborhood. When you stay silent, you miss chances to grow and enjoy real conversations. Before we begin, please subscribe to Corgi English Podcast and turn on the bell so you never miss our next episode. You do not have to speak fast or say big things today. One tiny step is enough to begin.

Words for Brave Speaking

Emma: Before we share our stories, let us look at six helpful words and phrases for today. These words describe how we feel when we try to speak English. First, nervous means feeling worried or a little afraid before you do something new or difficult. Second, mistake means something you say or do that is not right, which helps you learn. Third, brave means ready to do something hard even when you feel a little scared inside. Fourth, accent means the special way people from one place or country pronounce words when they speak. Fifth, mind goes blank means a moment when you suddenly forget what you wanted to say next. Sixth, confident means feeling sure that you can do something well without too much fear. Ryan, can you give us one clear sentence for each of these words?

Ryan: I would love to share some everyday examples with you. For nervous, you can say, Maya feels nervous before she calls a new client in English. For mistake, you can say, Tom made a small grammar mistake, and nobody cared at all. For brave, you can say, it was brave of Sara to ask a question during the team meeting. For accent, you can say, my work friend has a lovely accent, and I can understand every word she says. For mind goes blank, you can say, when the manager looks at me, my mind goes blank for a second. Finally, for confident, you can say, after practicing for two weeks, Leo feels more confident on the phone.

Why Your Brain Freezes

Emma: Many learners tell themselves that something is wrong with them when they freeze. Think about a normal moment in daily life. Maybe you are standing in line at a coffee shop, or you are sitting in a team meeting. You know the words in your head. You can read them and understand them easily. Then someone looks at you and waits for an answer. Suddenly, your heart beats fast, your hands feel warm, and your mind goes blank. You forget even the easiest words. You walk away feeling upset with yourself. Understanding why this happens is very important, because it helps you stop blaming yourself. Ryan, why does our brain freeze like that when we try to speak English?

Ryan: Your brain wants to protect you. When you feel nervous, your brain thinks you are in danger. It does not know the difference between a real threat and a friendly chat in English. So your brain sends a strong signal to stop and stay quiet. That is why your mind goes blank even when you know the words. Reading and listening feel safe because nobody is watching you. Speaking feels risky because another person is waiting right in front of you. Here is one simple tip to calm your brain down. Take one slow breath before you answer. Tell yourself that you are safe, and you only need three or four simple words.

Mistakes Are Totally Normal

Emma: That makes so much sense, Ryan. Now let us think about another big fear, the fear of making a mistake. Think of Maya. She works in a small office and needs to speak English sometimes. Last week, she wanted to tell her work friend about a broken printer. She thought about the sentence for five minutes because she wanted the grammar to be perfect. She felt so worried that she almost sent an email instead. Then she heard her work friend, who grew up speaking English, say something with the wrong word and laugh about it. Maya felt amazed and relieved. Ryan, why do we expect ourselves to be perfect when native speakers are not?

Ryan: Perfection is not the goal. Even people who spoke English as children make mistakes every single day. They forget a word, mix up two ideas, or pause in the middle of a sentence. Nobody stops the conversation to grade them. When you make a mistake in English, it simply means you are trying something brave. Most listeners only care about your message, not your grammar. If you say, yesterday I go to the store to buy milk, people still understand you completely. They know when you went and what you needed. Instead of waiting for a perfect sentence, aim for a clear message. Use short, simple words that you already know well, and let yourself learn as you go.

Confidence Comes After Action

Emma: I love that idea, Ryan. A clear message is always better than a silent mouth. Still, many people wait for a magical day when they will finally feel confident. Maybe you say to yourself, I will start speaking English next month after I finish this book. Or you think, I will join that speaking group next season when my English is much better. Months pass, you stay quiet, and you still feel scared. Waiting to feel ready keeps you stuck in the same place. It is a hard cycle to break, because fear makes us wait, and waiting makes the fear bigger. Ryan, how do we build real confidence if we do not feel it yet?

Ryan: Confidence comes after you speak. You cannot wait for confidence to arrive before you take action. Think of Leo. He wanted to speak English with his neighbors, but he always felt too shy. So he made a tiny plan. On Monday, he only said, good morning, to one neighbor by the front door. His voice was quiet, and he felt nervous, but he did it. On Wednesday, he asked, how is your week going? His neighbor smiled and answered kindly. After two weeks of these tiny steps, Leo realized he was not so scared anymore. His confidence grew because his brain saw that nothing bad happened. Start with one small sentence today, and let your confidence grow behind you.

Your Accent Is Fine

Emma: That story about Leo is so encouraging. One more thing stops many adults from speaking, and that is how their voice sounds. Maybe you listen to movies, shows, or podcasts and think, I do not sound like that at all. You hear your own accent during a call or a meeting, and you feel shy about it. You might worry that strangers or people at work will not respect you or understand your words. That worry can make you speak very softly or avoid talking altogether. Your voice is part of who you are, and this topic is very personal for many learners. Ryan, how should we think about our accent when we speak English?

Ryan: Clear words beat a perfect accent. Millions of people around the world speak English with different accents every day. Doctors, teachers, engineers, and business owners lead meetings and help clients while keeping accents from their home countries. An accent simply shows that you know more than one language, which is something to be proud of. People can understand you well when you speak slowly and clearly. You do not need to sound like a news host from London or New York. Focus on opening your mouth, speaking at a calm speed, and taking a short breath between ideas. When you slow down just a little, your accent sounds natural and easy to follow.

From Shyness to Daily Practice

Emma: Before we jump into our short scenes, we have a special note to share. The creator of this podcast is an English learner too and wrote a short note about today's topic. Here is what the creator wrote. I feel shy about speaking English because I think I am not good enough. I also worry about people judging me. And I do not have a clear topic to talk about. To build confidence, I name everyday objects and daily activities in English. And I practice speaking in front of a mirror before I talk to other people. These are small habits and changes today, but with patience and discipline, they will build a strong base for future success. Ryan, what do you think about these ideas from the creator?

Ryan: Those habits are wonderful and very smart. Maybe you feel just as shy when you do not know what to talk about. Naming everyday objects around your room is a great way to start because there is no pressure. Nobody is listening or judging you. Looking in a mirror helps you get used to seeing yourself speak English, which makes it feel normal. You can try two small things this week. First, when you make breakfast, say three things you see in your kitchen, like cup, bread, and warm water. Second, stand by the mirror for one minute and say what you will do today. Keep it simple, and stay patient with yourself.

A Short Voice Message

Emma: Now we are going to act out a short scene at work. I will play a new worker, and Ryan will play a helpful work friend. Hi Ryan, I just sent you a quick voice message about the schedule.

Ryan: Oh, great! Let me listen to it right now. Thanks for sending that over.

Emma: I felt a little nervous recording it, so I kept it very short. Did you understand the time for tomorrow?

Ryan: Yes, I understood you completely. You said ten o'clock in the main room, right?

Emma: Yes, that is right. I wanted to write a long email, but I decided to practice speaking instead.

Ryan: That was a brave choice. Your voice message was clear and easy to follow.

Emma: Thank you. My mind went blank for a second at the end, so I stopped talking.

Ryan: I did not even notice a problem. Taking a short pause actually sounds very calm and professional.

Emma: That makes me feel much better. Maybe I will send another voice message next week.

Ryan: Please do. Whenever you need to share a quick update, a short voice note works really well.

Phrases from the scene: A Short Voice Message

Emma: That was a great scene. In that conversation, the new worker did not wait until her English felt perfect. Instead of spending twenty minutes writing a long email, she sent a short voice message. When she felt nervous, she told her work friend honestly, and she got kind support back. One useful phrase from the scene was, I kept it very short. You can use this when you send a quick voice message or give a short answer at work. Another great phrase was, did you understand the time for tomorrow? This question helps you check that your message was clear. Ryan, what is another natural way to check if someone understood you?

Ryan: You can also ask, did that make sense, or, was that clear? Both of those questions sound very friendly and natural at work, on the phone, or with friends. Now let us practice two useful phrases from the scene out loud together. Speak clearly from wherever you are right now. Did you understand the time for tomorrow? Did you understand the time for tomorrow? Practicing these short lines out loud helps your mouth remember them when you feel nervous.

Chatting With a Neighbor

Emma: In our next scene, we are outside an apartment building. I will play a friendly neighbor, and Ryan will play an English learner. Morning! Lovely weather today, right? Are you heading out early for that big downtown event?

Ryan: Good morning! It is nice to see you. Sorry, could you say that again a little slower?

Emma: Oh, of course. I asked if you are going to the big event downtown this morning.

Ryan: Thank you for repeating that. No, I am just going to the grocery store today.

Emma: Nice! I went there yesterday, and they have fresh red apples on sale this week, by the way.

Ryan: Oh, good to know! I really like fresh apples. Are you having a busy morning today?

Emma: Not too busy. I am just walking the dog and grabbing some coffee. Have a wonderful day!

Ryan: Thank you, you too! Is that your dog? I see him at the bus stop every morning.

Emma: Yes, this is Max. He loves the bus stop because people always say hello to him. See you later!

Ryan: Bye, Max! See you tomorrow morning. And thank you again for repeating your question for me.

Phrases from the scene: Chatting With a Neighbor

Emma: What a warm and realistic moment! Sometimes people speak very fast, especially in casual talks outside near a busy street. In this scene, the learner did not pretend to understand or smile quietly and walk away. Instead, he stayed calm and asked his neighbor to repeat the question clearly. That kept the conversation friendly, relaxed, and easy. The most important phrase here was, sorry, could you say that again a little slower? You can use this polite line in any store, office, or short talk. Another great line was, thank you for repeating that. It shows respect and keeps the mood warm. Ryan, what is another polite way to ask someone to repeat something?

Ryan: You can say, sorry, I missed that, could you repeat it? You can also say, excuse me, what did you say? Those lines are short, polite, and very easy to remember when you feel nervous. Now let us say two key phrases from this scene out loud together. Speak up and repeat them right after me during the short breaks. Here is the first one. Sorry, could you say that again a little slower? Sorry, could you say that again a little slower? And here is the second one. Thank you for repeating that. Thank you for repeating that. Using these simple rescue lines will help you feel much safer in real daily conversations.

One Sentence at Work

Ryan: Good morning, Emma. Thanks for asking. It is going well, and I will finish it today.

Emma: That is great news, Ryan. Do you need any help from the design team before lunch?

Ryan: No, thank you, Emma. I have the files and the notes. I have everything I need right now.

Emma: Perfect, thank you. Just send me a quick note when the update is ready to check.

Ryan: Yes, I will finish the last part and send you a quick message early this afternoon.

Emma: Thanks so much, Ryan. Please tell me if anything changes later. Have a very productive morning, everyone!

Ryan: I will, Emma. If I find a problem with the website, I will tell you right away.

Emma: Sounds good to me. Let us all get back to work and finish our morning tasks now.

Ryan: Great. I will send you the finished update this afternoon. Talk to you later, everyone!

Phrases from the scene: One Sentence at Work

Emma: Look how simple and effective that was! In a team meeting, many learners think they need to give a long speech with lots of details. They feel scared because they try to plan five sentences in their head at once. In this scene, the worker gave one clear sentence to answer each question. He did not rush, and he sounded calm and ready. One very useful line from the scene is, it is going well, and I will finish it today. Another helpful line is, I have everything I need right now. You can use both of these lines in work meetings or school projects. Ryan, how else can someone answer a quick work question simply?

Ryan: You can say, I am still working on it, and I will update you this afternoon. That answer is honest, calm, and clear. Now let us practice two useful sentences from this meeting scene out loud together. Take a deep breath and speak clearly. I will say each one twice at a gentle speed so you can copy my voice in the pause. Here is the first sentence. It is going well, and I will finish it today. It is going well, and I will finish it today. Here is the second sentence. I have everything I need right now. I have everything I need right now. One short sentence is often all you need to succeed.

Five Minutes of Daily Speech

Emma: Now let us put everything into a simple daily plan that only takes five minutes. You do not need a partner to start speaking English every day. In the morning, while you make tea or coffee, say three things you see in your kitchen out loud. At lunch, look out the window for thirty seconds and describe the weather in one short sentence. In the afternoon, send yourself a one minute voice message on your phone about your day. Before bed, stand in front of the mirror and say one good thing that happened today. These tiny steps feel easy and safe. Ryan, how can our listeners keep doing these habits every day without giving up?

Ryan: Great question, Emma. First, make the habit so small that you cannot fail. If one minute feels too long, speak for just fifteen seconds. Second, connect your English habit to something you already do every day, like brushing your teeth or making morning coffee. That way, you will not forget it. Third, track your progress with a simple check mark on a paper calendar each night. Seeing a long row of check marks on your wall feels wonderful and keeps you going. Finally, try this daily English practice with our topic. Pick one short rescue phrase from this episode. Say it out loud three times before you leave home in the morning. Small, steady practice builds real strength.
