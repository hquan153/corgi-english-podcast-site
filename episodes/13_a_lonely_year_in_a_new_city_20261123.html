Do you feel lonely in a new city after moving for work or study? Follow Oliver through one quiet year as he changes his daily habits, joins an English group, and builds real connection.

Today's goal

After this episode, you can talk about moving to a new city, use words like lonely and belong, practice expressions like break the ice, and take daily steps to meet people.

From the creator

When I had to leave home to study in a new city, I knew nothing about that place. I felt completely lost and unsure, especially because I was all alone. I realized that this time was a base for my future. So I tried to change my lifestyle and daily habits to fit life alone in a completely unfamiliar city.

Key words

  • lonely: sad because you are alone and wish to be with other people
  • miss: to feel sad because a person or a place is far away
  • neighbor: a person who lives in a home next to or near your home
  • invite: to ask someone politely to come to a place or an event
  • join: to become a member of a group, class, or shared activity
  • belong: to feel happy, safe, and welcome as part of a group

Words for Life Away From Home

Before we meet Oliver, let's look at six helpful words that will appear in his story today. Take your time and listen closely to each meaning. First, lonely means sad because you are alone and wish to be with other people. Next, miss means to feel sad because a person or a place is far away. The third word is neighbor. Neighbor means a person who lives in a home next to or near your home. Fourth, invite means to ask someone in a friendly way to come to a place or an event. The fifth word is join. Join means to become a member of a group, class, or shared activity. Finally, belong means to feel happy, safe, and welcome as part of a group. A Quiet First Apartment

Oliver stood in the middle of his new apartment on a Tuesday night. Two brown boxes sat near the window, and the fridge held only water and bread. He had moved to this big city for a good office job, and everyone told him he was lucky. Still, as the street lights came on outside, the room felt huge and empty. Oliver opened his window to hear the buses below, just to feel less alone. Back home, his family always talked in the kitchen while dinner cooked. Here, the only sound was the hum of the old fridge. He ate a simple sandwich on the floor because he did not have a table yet. Oliver felt proud of his brave move, yet his chest felt tight with quiet sadness.

Have you ever sat in a new room and felt that sudden silence? Many people think a good job or a nice city will make you happy right away. In truth, the first weeks in a new place are often the hardest part. Your brain is busy learning new streets, new work rules, and new faces every hour. At the same time, your old friends and family are not nearby to share your day. Feeling lonely at the start is a very normal human reaction, not a sign that you made a mistake. One simple idea helps during that first week. Bring two small things from home, like a favorite cup or a warm blanket. When your room feels a little familiar, your mind can rest more easily after a long day.

Missing Home Every Evening

During his second week, Oliver found a daily rhythm at work, yet the evenings hit him hard. Every afternoon at six, he walked out of his office building into a crowd of strangers. Everyone seemed to know where they were going, and nobody looked up. On his walk back, Oliver passed a bakery that smelled like warm cinnamon. That small smell made him miss his hometown so deeply that he stopped on the sidewalk. He remembered walking past his old neighborhood shop with his brother every Friday. Back at his apartment, Oliver called his mom, but she was busy cooking, so they talked for only five minutes. When he hung up, the quiet room felt even colder. Oliver realized that he did not just miss people. He missed his whole old life.

When you miss home, one small smell can bring big feelings. Even a short song can bring back strong memories. It's natural to feel that way in a new place. Your whole world has changed all at once. Think about your old day back home. So much of it ran on easy, familiar habits. You knew where to buy fresh bread each morning. You knew where to sit on the bus. You knew who to call late at night. In a new city, every small choice takes extra thought. Making all those choices can make you feel tired. That's why one or two old routines help so much. Maybe you always make hot tea at seven. Maybe you play your favorite music while you cook. Keep those small, healthy habits from home with you. They give you a steady floor while you build a new life.

Silent Weekends Alone

Monday through Friday kept Oliver busy at his desk. He thought he was doing fine. Then Saturday morning arrived with two empty days ahead. On his fourth Saturday, Oliver stayed in bed until eleven. There was nowhere he needed to go. He made instant noodles for lunch in his tiny kitchen. Then he sat on his sofa and looked at his phone. He saw bright photos of old friends eating together back home. His heart sank a little bit. Outside his window, people walked in pairs toward the park. The afternoon sun looked bright on the street. Oliver promised himself he would go out after lunch. Then he waited until three, and soon it was dark. He had not spoken one word out loud for two days. His voice sounded strange when he cleared his throat.

Look at what happened to Oliver on that quiet weekend. When you feel lonely, staying inside feels safe at first. Looking at your phone feels easy too. However, bright photos of other people can make you feel worse. Watching them from a dark room hurts your mood. Spending time around people helps protect your daily energy. When we stay silent for days, our thoughts grow heavy and slow. You don't need a big party to fix a quiet weekend. Give yourself one small reason to go out on Saturday morning. Walk to a market to buy some fresh fruit. Sit on a park bench and read a short book. You can also spend an hour in a busy library. Being around people reminds you that real neighbors share your city.

Trying Too Hard Once

After two months, Oliver decided he needed to fix his problem fast. One Friday, someone at work sent an email to invite the whole office to a big party downtown. Oliver put on his nicest shirt and promised himself he would make three new friends that night. When he arrived, the room was loud, crowded, and full of people who already knew each other. Everyone held a drink and laughed in tight circles. Oliver stood near the snack table for twenty minutes, smiling nicely while nobody came over to talk. He tried to join one group near the wall, yet the loud music hid their words. After forty minutes, Oliver felt invisible in a room of fifty people. He quietly slipped out the side door and walked home feeling foolish.

Have you ever felt even more lonely inside a big, noisy crowd? That happens to many adults, especially when they move to a new place. Big parties look exciting in movies, yet they are often terrible places to start real friendships. When a room is loud and people already stand in close groups, starting a natural chat feels almost impossible. If you tried a big event and went home early, please don't blame yourself. You are not bad at talking to people. You simply chose a hard setting for your first step. Most strong friendships begin in calm, smaller places where you see the same person more than once. Next time, skip the huge loud party if it drains you. Look for quiet spaces with one or two people instead.

A Bad Rainy Sunday

The hardest moment of the year came on a gray Sunday in late autumn. Cold rain tapped against Oliver's glass window from morning until late afternoon. He had lived there for four months, and his phone stayed silent all weekend. At four in the afternoon, the sky was already dark, and Oliver sat on his bed wrapped in a blanket. He felt tired, small, and ready to pack his bags and move back home. For a long hour, he stared at his suitcase in the corner of the room. Then he asked himself one honest question. If he stayed inside waiting for someone to invite him out, how many more months would pass? Nobody in this huge city knew he was waiting. Oliver realized that if things were going to change, he had to start small himself.

Low days like Oliver's rainy Sunday happen to almost everyone who starts over somewhere new. Sometimes heavy sadness stays for many weeks and makes daily life hard. When that happens, talking to a doctor is always a wise step. For normal lonely weekends, though, that low point can also bring an important wake-up call. In a big city, people are not being mean when they walk past you. They are simply busy with their own work, shopping, and daily plans. Waiting for a stranger to knock on your door and become your best friend almost never works. Connection usually begins when you take one gentle step outward. Think about your own week right now. Is there one small place where you can show up regularly, instead of waiting at home for things to change?

Changing His Daily Routine

On Monday morning, Oliver made a very small rule for himself. He did not try to make ten friends or go to big parties anymore. Instead, he decided to leave his apartment for thirty minutes every afternoon right after work. He walked down the same street and stopped at a small corner coffee shop near the train station. On the first day, Oliver bought a cup of tea, sat near the window, and read a short book. Nobody talked to him, and that was fine. He came back on Wednesday, and again on Friday, at the exact same time. By the second week, the worker behind the counter looked up and smiled when Oliver walked through the door. That tiny smile did not solve everything, yet Oliver walked home feeling a little lighter.

Why did Oliver's small coffee shop habit help so much? In daily life, we feel safer around faces we see again and again. Think about how you notice the same bus driver or the same person walking a dog every morning. Even before you know their name, seeing a familiar face makes a strange street feel warmer. Many people try a new place once, feel shy, and never return. If you visit the same park or market at the same time each week, people start to recognize you too. You don't even have to start a long conversation on day one. Just become a regular face somewhere near your home. When someone finally smiles and says good morning, your new city starts to feel a little more friendly.

A Smile From a Neighbor

One week later, Oliver carried two heavy bags of groceries up the stairs to his apartment. At the top landing, his neighbor opened the door across the hall at the exact same moment. She was an older woman with silver hair and a bright yellow umbrella. Usually, Oliver just looked down at his keys and walked quickly inside. This time, he stopped, smiled, and said, "Good evening, it's cold out today." The woman smiled back and answered that the wind felt strong near the river. She told him her name was Grace, and Oliver shared his name too. They talked for only one minute about the weather and the stairs. When Oliver closed his own door, he stood there smiling for a moment. Saying hello to one neighbor felt surprisingly good.

A one-minute chat in a hallway might look tiny, yet it changes how you feel about coming home. When you know even one neighbor by name, your building stops feeling like a cold box of strangers. You don't need to invite someone into your home right away or ask deep questions. A simple smile and a short line about the rain or the elevator is plenty. Many people stay quiet because they worry about bothering others, or because they feel shy about their English. Remember that most neighbors enjoy a quick, polite hello too. Tomorrow, when you pass someone near your front door or on the stairs, try looking up from your phone. Say a simple good morning. Small warm moments like that slowly turn a lonely building into a much nicer place.

Joining an English Class

That small win with Grace gave Oliver a bit more courage. Two weeks later, he saw a paper sign on the window of his favorite coffee shop. A local library nearby held a free English conversation club every Thursday evening. Oliver felt nervous because his spoken English was still simple, and he often forgot words. Still, he wanted to practice speaking and meet people who understood life in a new place. On Thursday night, Oliver walked into a bright room with ten chairs set in a circle. Six adults sat there with pens and cups of water, and everyone looked a little shy at first. The leader welcomed Oliver with a warm smile and asked him to join the circle. When it was his turn, Oliver spoke slowly about his job and his quiet apartment. Everyone smiled and listened kindly.

Look at what happened when Oliver decided to join that Thursday group. Speaking English did more than help his grammar and listening skills. It gave him a shared room with other adults who were also brave enough to try something new. If you are learning English right now, your language goal can become a wonderful bridge to new people. When you join a class or a study club, you already share a clear goal with everyone there. Nobody expects you to speak fast or use big, difficult words. People in a learning group know how hard it feels to look for the right word. You can start by sharing one short sentence about your day. Practicing English with others turns a lonely evening into a shared hour of growth.

Saying Yes to Coffee

Oliver kept going to the English club every Thursday for a month. Soon, he started sitting next to a friendly man named Sam, who had also moved to the city for work. They often laughed together when they both forgot the same English word. After the fifth class, the group packed up their bags and walked outside into the cool evening air. Sam turned to Oliver on the library steps to invite him for a quick coffee across the street. For one second, Oliver felt tired and thought about going straight home to his quiet sofa. Then he remembered that lonely rainy Sunday from months ago. He took a breath, smiled, and said yes. They sat for forty minutes, talked in simple English, and shared two warm cakes.

Saying yes when you feel tired can be hard. Often, you feel a bit nervous inside too. When someone tries to invite us for coffee, we pause. Our first thought is often to stay at home. Staying home feels easy and safe. We worry we will run out of things to say. We worry our English will sound bad to others. Yet real friendships grow out of forty simple minutes after a class or a meeting. You don't need to plan a huge dinner. You don't need to know a thousand jokes. When someone opens a small door, say yes to thirty minutes. Tell yourself you can leave after one cup of coffee. Most of the time, those minutes feel warm and light.

Feeling a Sense of Belonging

One year after moving to the city, Oliver sat by his apartment window on another Sunday afternoon. His apartment now held a small wooden table, two bright plants, and a framed photo from home. Earlier that morning, he had waved to his neighbor Grace while carrying fresh bread from the corner shop. On Tuesday, he was going to meet Sam for lunch near their offices. Oliver still had quiet evenings by himself, and he still missed his family when big holidays came around. The difference was inside him. He no longer felt like a lost stranger watching life from behind glass. When he walked down his street, people knew his face, and he knew where to go. Slowly, through many small steps, Oliver realized he had started to belong.

Notice that Oliver's life did not turn into a loud movie overnight. He did not suddenly have fifty close friends, and he still missed his family on special days. That honest picture is important to remember. Building a life in a new city takes time, often many months of small, quiet choices. You don't need to fix your whole social life this week. What matters is taking one steady step, and then another, until the unfamiliar streets start to feel like yours. One friendly hello, one afternoon walk, and one shared hour in a class can slowly change your whole year. Be gentle with yourself if things move slowly. Every small effort you make today helps you belong tomorrow. Give yourself credit for every single day you keep trying.

Daily Steps to Meet People

Now let's look at what Oliver does each day. You can copy his simple habits today or tomorrow. In the morning, look up from your phone outside. Say a warm good morning to a neighbor near your door. After lunch, step outside for ten minutes of fresh air. Do not eat every meal alone at your desk. In the late afternoon, walk along the same short street. Visit the same quiet shop each week. Soon, the people there will start to recognize you. In the evening, send one kind message to a friend. You can also join a small group class once a week. Finally, before bed, write down one good moment from your day. Write it on a small sheet of paper. These small actions keep you connected to the world around you.

Keeping new habits works best when they are easy to start. First, begin with one tiny step each day. For example, say hello to just one person. Do not try to change everything at once. Second, prepare the night before you go to sleep. Put your walking shoes and keys near the door. Then stepping outside will take much less effort. Third, track your progress on a paper calendar. Draw a small star each time you step outside. Next, spend ten minutes a day on English practice. Speak out loud about your new neighborhood at home. Describe the street, the weather, or a friendly neighbor. Use simple sentences you already know. Then real talks outside will feel much easier. Small, steady steps always beat big, hard plans.
