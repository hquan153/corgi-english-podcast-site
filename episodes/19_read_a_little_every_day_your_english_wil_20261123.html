Read a Little English Every Single Day | Corgi English Podcast

Do you start an English book and stop after a few pages? Learn how to read English every day with easy books, calm habits, and simple listening tips.

Today's goal

After this episode, you can choose an easy English book, build a short daily reading habit, and talk about your reading without common mistakes.

A common problem and a tip

You open an English book at night, and you see ten new words on the first page. Soon you feel tired, close the book, and put it away. Tomorrow morning, try reading just one easy page while you drink your tea. Stop after five minutes, even if you want to keep going.

Key words

  • graded reader: a short book written in simple English for people who are learning the language
  • underline: to draw a straight line under an important word or sentence in your book
  • guess the meaning: to think about what a new word means by looking at the words around it
  • chapter: one main part of a book, often with its own number or short title
  • habit: something small that you do regularly at the same time almost every single day
  • read out loud: to say the words on a page with your voice so you can hear them

Useful expressions

  • “a page-turner”: a very interesting book that you do not want to put down
  • “read between the lines”: to understand a hidden meaning that the writer does not say directly
  • “from cover to cover”: to read a whole book from the first page to the last page
  • “lose yourself in a book”: to enjoy a story so much that you forget everything around you

Common mistakes

  • Many learners buy a famous, difficult novel and quit after ten pages because every sentence feels too hard. → Pick a short graded reader or an easy story so you can enjoy the plot and finish the book.
  • Some readers look up every unknown word in a dictionary and quickly lose the story. → Keep reading to the end of the page, and only check a word if it stops you from understanding.
  • Many learners say "I am reading this book since two weeks" and pronounce past "read" like "reed". → Say "I have been reading this book for two weeks" and pronounce the past tense "read" like the color "red".

Read and Listen Together

Checking Every Single Word

Emma: Here is the second common mistake. Some readers look up every unknown word in a dictionary and quickly lose the story. Think of David. Ryan: Skip first, check later. Your goal while reading is to follow the story, not to finish a dictionary. When you meet an unknown word, keep reading to the end of the page. Most of the time, you can guess the meaning from the rest of the sentence. If you only understand most of a paragraph, that is already enough to enjoy it. Only stop and open a dictionary if one word blocks the whole meaning of the scene. Or better yet, underline the word and check it after you finish the chapter. You will stay inside the story and read much faster. And you will actually remember the few words you look up later. That feels so much lighter.

Mixing Up Read and Reading

Emma: Our third mistake is an English mistake learners often make when they talk about their reading habit. Think of Claire. Claire was talking to a friend over lunch about a great graded reader in her bag. She wanted to tell her friend two things. First, she started reading the book two weeks ago and she is still reading it today. Second, she finished chapter three yesterday evening. When Claire spoke, she mixed up two common English forms. She used the wrong tense and the wrong time word before two weeks. She also pronounced the past form of read the exact same way as the present form. Ryan, how can learners say and pronounce these reading sentences the right way?

Ryan: Let's fix both quickly. First, think about an action that started earlier and is still happening now. Many learners say, "I am reading this book since two weeks." Instead, use "have been" and "for" with a length of time. Listen carefully and repeat this correct sentence. "I have been reading this book for two weeks." Let's say it clearly one more time. "I have been reading this book for two weeks." Second, notice the verb read. The present and the past tense look identical on the page, but they sound different. Today, you read a chapter, sounding like "reed". Yesterday, you read a chapter, sounding exactly like the color "red". Practice saying, "I read two pages yesterday," with that clear "red" sound.
