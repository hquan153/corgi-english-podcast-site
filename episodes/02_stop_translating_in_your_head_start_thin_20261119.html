Start Thinking in English Every Day! | Corgi English Podcast

Do you translate every sentence in your head before you speak? Learn how to think in English with small, easy games for a normal day, in slow and clear English for A2 to B1 learners.

▶ Watch this episode on YouTube (turn on the subtitles, then use this page while you listen)

Today's goal

After this episode, you can describe simple things around you in English without translating first.

From the creator

English is my second language, and my head is full of Vietnamese words. So my first habit is to translate from Vietnamese when I want to say or write something in English. It is slow and not very correct, and with all my um sounds, the other person thinks my English is weak. My tip is to practice thinking in English and to stop translating from Vietnamese. Even simple thoughts about your daily routine are enough, but you must keep going.

Key words

  • translate: to change words from one language into another language
  • in your head: inside your mind, when you think but do not speak
  • describe: to say what something or someone is like, using words
  • label: to give something a name, or to put a name on it
  • habit: something you do again and again, often without thinking about it
  • out loud: in a voice that people can hear, not only in your mind

Useful expressions

  • “old habits die hard”: it is difficult to stop doing something you have done for a long time
  • “one step at a time”: slowly, doing one small part and then the next one
  • “get the hang of it”: learn how to do something well after some practice
  • “think on your feet”: think and answer quickly, without time to prepare

Full transcript

Caught Between Two Languages

Emma: Hello, everyone, and welcome to Corgi English Podcast! This is a calm place for beginner and intermediate learners, with slow and clear English about real life. Let me start with a small question for you. When someone asks you something in English, what happens inside your mind? Do you hear the answer in your own language first? And then do you change it into English, one word at a time? This is very common, and it can feel slow and hard. Today, we try a different way. After this episode, you can describe simple things around you in English, without translating first. We will use small games for a normal day, and each one takes only a minute or two. My co-host Ryan is here with me, as always. Ryan, come and say hello to everyone!

Ryan: Hello, Emma, and a big hello to every English thinker out there! I really like this topic, because it touches almost every learner. Translating in your head is like carrying a heavy dictionary everywhere you go. It helps a little, but it makes every step slow. This matters in real life, because real conversations move fast. Someone at work asks a quick question, or a neighbor says something friendly at the door. If your answer takes a long trip through another language, the moment is often gone. So today, we want to make that trip much shorter. Before we start, here is a small request. Subscribe to Corgi English Podcast and tap the bell, and new words will come to you with every episode. Now, let us leave the heavy dictionary at home and start thinking in English together.

A Quick Question at Work

Emma: Now, a short scene to start. I play a new team member in a busy office, and Ryan plays my team leader. Here we go. Good morning! You wanted to see me?

Ryan: Good morning! Yes, just a quick question. The customer from yesterday called again. Can you describe the problem with her order?

Emma: Oh, yes. The order. Um, sorry, give me a second. I know the answer, but I am translating every word in my head first.

Ryan: That is okay, really. Forget the perfect sentence. Just use short, simple words. What was wrong with the order?

Emma: Okay. Simple words. The box came late. It was open. And one cup inside the box was broken.

Ryan: See? That is very clear. Now I understand the whole problem. What does the customer want us to do?

Emma: She wants a new cup. She does not want her money back. She was friendly, but a little tired of waiting.

Ryan: Good. Then let us send a new cup today. Can you call her back and tell her that, out loud, just like you told me?

Emma: Yes, I can do that. Short sentences feel much easier. I think I can stop translating and just say it.

Ryan: Exactly. You knew all the words already. Call her before lunch, and then come and tell me how it went.

Words for an English Mind

Emma: Before we go deeper, let us learn six useful words for today. They help you talk about what happens in your mind when you use English. Listen slowly, and say them quietly with me if you like. Our first word is translate. Translate means to change words from one language into another. Next, we have in your head. In your head means inside your mind, when you think but do not speak. The third word is describe. Describe means to say what something or someone is like. Then comes label. Label means to give something a name, or to put a name on it. Habit means something you do again and again, often without thinking. And the last one is out loud. Out loud means in a voice that people can hear. Ryan, can you show us each word in a real sentence?

Ryan: With pleasure, and I will keep them short and simple. A few of them come from our little office scene. Translate. The new woman at the office tried to translate every word before she answered. In your head. Before an important phone call, some people practice the whole conversation in their head. Describe. The team leader asked her to describe the problem with the order. Label. Please label each box with the name of the customer. Habit. Checking the phone in bed is a habit for a lot of people. Out loud. Read the new words out loud, so your mouth learns them too. And there you have it, six words for one big idea. Keep your ears open, because every one of them will come back again in this episode.

Why We Translate First

Emma: Let us begin with the reason behind all this. Picture a simple moment. You see a cat on a wall, and you want to say it in English. But first, your mind says it in your own language. Then you look for the English words, one by one. It all happens so fast that you hardly notice it. Maybe you even feel a little angry with yourself when you catch it. Why is this important? Because it is hard to change a habit we do not understand. When we know why something happens, we can stop blaming ourselves. And then we can choose a better path. So, Ryan, why does the mind go to the first language before it goes to English?

Ryan: It is the road your mind knows best. Think about it. You have used your first language every day since you were a baby. Thousands of words live there, and the paths to them are wide and easy. English words are newer, so the paths to them are still small. When you need a word quickly, your mind takes the big road first. That is normal, and it does not mean you are a bad learner. In fact, at the very start, a little translating can help you understand new words. The problem comes later, when the habit stays. So here is one tip for this week. When you learn a new word, do not only write the translation next to it. Write a short English sentence or draw a tiny picture too. That way, the English word gets its own path.

What Translating Costs You

Emma: So translating is normal. But it also has a price, and that price grows in real conversations. Think about a phone call with a stranger. They ask you a question and wait. You build the answer in your own language, then change it piece by piece. The silence feels very long, maybe longer for you than for them. Your heart beats a little faster, and you forget even easy words. And sometimes the final sentence still sounds strange, even with correct words. This matters, because it changes how we feel about speaking. A slow answer can make us feel small, and then we speak even less. Ryan, what does translating really cost us when we are in a conversation?

Ryan: Two things, time and meaning. Let me tell you about Tom. Tom is standing at the counter of a busy coffee shop in a new city. The young man behind the counter asks, "For here or to go?" Tom understands every word, but his mind starts to translate. Here? Go? Go where? The line behind him grows, and his face gets hot. Finally, he says, "Yes, please." The young man smiles and waits. Tom feels a little silly. Later, Tom learns that some phrases do not work word by word. To go just means that you take the coffee with you. So now he learns phrases like that as one piece, together with the situation. The next week, he hears the same question and answers in one second. That is the lesson. Learn groups of words that go together, and your answers come faster.

Name the Things Around You

Emma: Now for the fun part, our first small game. Look around you right now. Maybe you see a cup, a window, a phone, or a bag. Do you know all of them in English? Most learners know simple words like these very well. But often, the mind still reaches them through the first language. If we connect the English word straight to the thing, we skip one step. And that step is where the time goes. It sounds like a tiny change, but it happens hundreds of times a day. So saving one small step each time really adds up. Ryan, how can someone start doing this at home, with the things they see every day?

Ryan: Name it in English, straight away. Here is a nice example. Think of Ella. She is a young mother with two small kids and very little free time. One Sunday evening, she decides to try a label week at home. She writes English words on small pieces of paper. Fridge. Mirror. Window. Spoon. She puts them on the things in her kitchen and bathroom. All week, she sees the words while she makes tea or brushes her teeth. At first, it feels a little silly, and her kids laugh at the notes. But by Thursday, something changes. She looks at the fridge, and the English word comes first. Ella feels a small, quiet pride. So try this yourself. Choose five things in one room and label them today. When the words come easily, move the notes to a new room.

Small Sentences on the Way

Emma: Single words are a great start, but we do not speak in single words. So the next step is small sentences. Imagine your walk to work, or the bus ride home. Usually, the mind is busy with plans and worries and lists of things to do. Now, try this. Use five of those minutes to describe the world around you in English. Nobody can hear you, so nobody can judge you. It is free practice, and you can do it every single day. You do not need a book, a teacher, or a phone. All you need is your eyes and a few simple words. Ryan, what kind of sentences should a learner make on a walk like that?

Ryan: Keep them short, and keep them in the present. Sentences about right now are perfect for this game. You do not need long, clever sentences. You need sentences that you can make in two seconds. For example, on your way to work, you can think, "The sky is gray today. A man is walking his dog. The bus is late again. I feel a little cold." Do you see? Each sentence is small, but it is real English, made in your own mind. Nobody translated it. Here is my one strong tip for this game. Do not stop to look up a word you do not know. Just skip it, or use an easier word, and keep going. The goal is speed, not perfect English. Often, after a week or two, these little sentences start to come more quickly.

Talk to Yourself Out Loud

Emma: Our next idea sounds a little strange at first. Talk to yourself. Yes, really, and out loud. Some people feel shy about this, even when they are alone at home. They think it looks silly, or a bit crazy. But talking to yourself is very common, and many people do it every day without thinking. Maybe you already talk to yourself in your first language when you look for your keys. So why not in English? For a learner, it is also a great way to practice. Your thoughts become real words. Your mouth gets used to English sounds, and nobody is there to correct you. Ryan, where and when can people do this without feeling strange about it?

Ryan: Private places are perfect for it. Think of Grace. She drives to work for forty minutes every morning, alone in her car. One day, she decides to use that time. She tells herself, out loud, what she plans to do at work. "First, I will answer my emails. Then I will call the bank." At red lights, she describes the street. "A woman is crossing with a big umbrella." The first few days feel funny, and she laughs at herself. But soon, her mouth moves more easily, and English words feel less heavy. There is a good reason for this. Saying a word out loud often helps you remember it better than reading it silently. So find your own private place. The shower, the car, or a walk with headphones all work well. Nobody needs to hear you.

When a Word Is Missing

Emma: Now, a problem that every learner meets sooner or later. You are thinking or speaking in English, and suddenly one word is missing. You know it in your own language, but not in English. At that moment, the old habit comes back very fast. You stop, you translate, and maybe you reach for your phone. And the whole conversation stops with you. The other person waits, and the silence grows. It is not a nice feeling, and maybe you know that moment well. This is important, because it happens to learners at every level, even very good ones. The question is what to do in that moment. Ryan, what can we do when the English word just is not there?

Ryan: Walk around the missing word. You do not need the perfect word. You can describe the idea with simple words that you already know. Say you want the word kettle, but it does not come. Just say, "the thing I use to make hot water for tea." People understand you, and the talk goes on. This is a real speaking skill. Good speakers use it all the time, even in their first language. It also keeps you inside English, because you think with English words you already have. Later, when you are free, look up the missing word. Write it down with a short English sentence. Next time, it will be there for you. A few useful phrases help here too, such as "it is a kind of" or "it is something you use for". These little phrases can save a whole conversation.

From Translating to Thinking

Emma: And now, a special part of our show. The creator of this podcast is an English learner too. For this episode, the creator sent us a short note about this exact problem. I want to read it to you just as it is. Here is what the creator wrote. "English is my second language, and my head is full of Vietnamese words. So my first habit is to translate from Vietnamese when I want to say or write something in English. It is slow and not very correct, and with all my um sounds, the other person thinks my English is weak. My tip is to practice thinking in English and to stop translating from Vietnamese. Even simple thoughts about your daily routine are enough, but you must keep going." Ryan, what do you think about this honest note?

Ryan: I really like it, because it is honest and very practical. Look at the last part. The creator does not say that we need special books or long classes. Simple thoughts about a daily routine are enough. That is good news, because everybody has a routine. And the two words at the end, keep going, are the real key. Maybe you feel the same way as the creator. Maybe you also hear yourself say um, and your face gets warm. So here are two small ideas for this week. First, pick one routine, like making coffee, and think it in English every day. "I fill the cup. I add the milk. I wait." Second, when you catch yourself translating, do not get angry. Just smile, notice it, and say the sentence again in simple English.

Who Is Thinking in English?

Emma: It is quiz time, and today it is all about thinking habits. Here is how our game works. I describe a real situation, and you think about it for a moment yourself. Then Ryan shares his opinion. Our first person is Kenji from Japan. Kenji works in sales, and he writes many emails in English. For every email, he first writes the whole message in Japanese. Then he puts it into a translation app and sends the result. Each email takes him a long time, and some of them sound a little strange to his clients. So, take a moment and think. Is Kenji helping his English, or not? What can he do better? Okay, Ryan, now it is your turn. What do you make of Kenji and his emails?

Ryan: Kenji works hard, but this habit keeps his English small. Every email starts in Japanese, so his mind never practices building English sentences. The app does the thinking for him. And apps sometimes choose words that sound strange in a work email. So, here is what Kenji can do instead. He can start with short emails only and write them straight in English, with simple sentences. He can keep a small list of useful email phrases, like "Thank you for your message" or "I will send it today." And he can use the app only to check one word, not to write the whole email. His emails will be slower at first, but they will sound more like him. Over a few months, writing in English can feel much more natural.

Emma: That sounds like a plan he can really follow. Now, two more people. Sofia from Mexico keeps a tiny English diary. Every night before bed, she writes one line about her day, in very simple words. Last night, she wrote, "I cooked rice and called my sister." When she does not know a word, she describes it with easier words. Our last person is Ahmed from Egypt. Ahmed knows hundreds of English words from lists. But he always studies them with the Arabic word next to each one. In meetings, he understands a lot. Yet when he wants to answer, he goes quiet and searches for words in his head. So, Ryan, what do you think about Sofia and Ahmed?

Ryan: Sofia is doing a wonderful job. One line a night sounds tiny, but it is a real habit. She thinks in English, she uses words she knows, and she goes around missing words instead of stopping. That is just what we talked about today. My only advice for her is to say her line out loud too. Ahmed has a different problem. He has many words, but each word is tied to Arabic, not to real life. So in a meeting, every word needs a translation first. Ahmed can learn each new word with an English example sentence. He can say his words out loud while he looks at the real thing. And he can practice a few short answers before each meeting. So here is the main lesson. Connect English words to your life, not to your first language.

A Day Full of English Thoughts

Emma: Now let us build a simple plan for one full day. These are tiny actions, and each one fits into normal life. In the morning, at breakfast, name five things on the table in English. Cup, bread, egg, whatever you see. On the way to work or school, describe three things you see in short sentences. After lunch, think about your plan for the afternoon in English. Two or three sentences are enough. In the evening, while you cook or wash the dishes, talk to yourself out loud for one minute. And before bed, write one line in English about your day. That is five small moments, and maybe ten minutes in total. Ryan, how can we keep doing this when life gets busy?

Ryan: Make it so small that it feels too easy. That is my first tip. On a busy day, one moment is enough, maybe just the diary line. Small actions are easy to repeat, and repeating builds the habit. My second tip is to link each moment to something you already do. Breakfast, the bus, brushing your teeth. The old habit reminds you of the new one. Third, keep a simple record. Put a small mark on a calendar for every day you do it. A line of marks feels good, and it helps you continue. And here is one more tip, for your listening. Play this episode again tomorrow, and pause after a sentence you like. Say it out loud, and then make one new sentence of your own about your day.

For example, old habits die hard, so be patient when you still translate sometimes. The next one is one step at a time. It means slowly, doing one small part and then the next one. Ryan: Now it is your turn to speak. I will say four sentences slowly. After each one, say it out loud, in your own voice. If you are on a bus, you can just whisper it. Here is the first one. Your Thoughts, in English

Emma: What a useful talk this was. So, what can you take with you today? Translating in your head is normal, because your first language is the road your mind knows best. But it costs time, and word by word, some phrases come out strange. The answer is to give English its own roads. Label the things around you. Describe your walk in short sentences. Talk to yourself out loud in private. And when a word is missing, walk around it. Remember Kenji and his emails, Sofia and her tiny diary, and Ahmed in his meetings. Now, a small challenge for you.
