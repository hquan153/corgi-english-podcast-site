The Perfect Moment Never Came: A Story About Starting | Corgi English Podcast

Are you waiting for the perfect moment to begin a new goal or practice English? Follow Daniel's story to learn simple ways to take your first small step today.

Today's goal

After this episode, you can use six common words about starting and describe simple daily steps to take action before you feel ready.

From the creator

When I first learned English, I always imagined using English easily and well. In reality, I was afraid to use English and afraid to sit in class listening to an English teacher. Everything needs a start, and your start can be completely different from what you imagined. Do not give up, because this is only the first difficulty in your learning journey. Try to get past it, because many more challenges and exciting things wait ahead for you.

Key words

  • put off: to decide to do something later instead of doing it right now
  • hesitate: to pause and wait before acting because you feel unsure or worried
  • regret: to feel sad later about something you did or did not do
  • waste: to use your time or energy in a way that helps nothing
  • on time: arriving or happening at the right hour and not too late
  • finally: after a long wait or after many delays and hard tries

Useful expressions

  • “get the ball rolling”: to do the first small thing that starts a plan or activity
  • “no time like the present”: right now is the best moment to start instead of waiting
  • “better late than never”: it is still good to start even if you waited a long time

The Notebook on the Shelf

On a cool Sunday afternoon, Daniel walked into a quiet book store near his apartment. He picked out a thick blue notebook and two smooth black pens. At home, he placed the notebook carefully on his kitchen table and smiled. Tomorrow, he told himself, he was going to write down his big plans for the year. He wanted to exercise more, cook healthy dinners, and speak better English at work. That evening, Daniel felt proud just looking at the clean blue cover. However, when Monday morning came, he rushed to catch his bus and left the notebook on the table. After work, he felt tired, so he put the notebook on a high shelf. He told himself he needed a calm, free evening to start properly. Days passed, and the blue notebook stayed closed and clean on that shelf.

Do you recognize that feeling when you buy new supplies for a new habit? Buying a notebook or nice shoes feels exciting, because our minds love fresh beginnings. However, waiting for a completely calm evening often means we never open the book at all. Many adults wait until they feel confident, rested, and totally ready before they begin. The honest truth is that feeling ready rarely comes before you start. Confidence usually grows after you take action, not while you sit and wait. Think about one goal sitting on your own shelf right now. Maybe it's an English book, a gym bag, or an online course you bought months ago. You don't need a special afternoon with zero stress to begin. Opening the book for just two minutes tonight is already a real start.

Waiting for Monday Morning

Two weeks later, Daniel stood in his living room on a Wednesday night. He looked at his running shoes by the front door and felt sad. He had missed his morning walk again because heavy rain fell early in the day. Instead of going out for a short walk after dinner, he made a neat rule in his head. Starting next Monday, he promised himself, he was going to walk thirty minutes every single morning. Right away, Daniel felt lighter and happier. He poured a cup of tea and watched a show on his sofa. Making a plan for Monday felt wonderful because there was no sweat and no struggle tonight. When Monday arrived, though, Daniel woke up late and skipped the walk again. Soon, he pushed his fresh start to the first day of next month.

Notice why Daniel felt so happy on that Wednesday night. When you put off a task until next Monday, your brain feels relief right away. You get the warm feeling of planning without doing any hard work today. That's why fake start dates like Monday morning or January first are so popular. They look neat on a calendar, but life on Monday is usually just as busy as Wednesday. If you wait for a clean, easy week, you will probably waste many weeks waiting. Here is a small idea you can try today. Whenever you hear yourself say, I will start next Monday, stop for five seconds. Ask yourself what tiny piece you can do today, even on a messy Wednesday afternoon. Ten minutes in the evening still counts, and it breaks the habit of waiting.

A Friend Just Starts

On Saturday morning, Daniel met his friend Hannah at a cafe. The small cafe stood right near the train station. Hannah placed a brown paper bag on the table. Inside were cheap brushes and a thin pad of paper. Daniel asked why she bought such simple tools. Why did she not wait for a nicer set? Hannah laughed and told him about her week. She joined a beginner painting group at the community center. She knew almost nothing about colors or drawing. On the first day, she felt very nervous. Still, she walked in and painted a crooked yellow cup. Daniel drank his coffee slowly as he listened. He had spent months planning the perfect start. Meanwhile, Hannah already had fun and learned something new. Walking home, he kept thinking about her crooked yellow cup.

Why did Hannah move forward while Daniel stayed stuck at home? Hannah did not wait to become good before she began. She accepted that her first try was going to look messy and simple. Many people hesitate because they compare their Day One to someone else's Year Five. When you see a finished painting or hear a fluent speaker, you forget all their early days full of mistakes. Every skilled person once stood at the very beginning with simple tools and nervous hands. Have you ever stopped yourself from trying a hobby because you did not want to look like a beginner? You don't have to buy expensive gear or read ten books first. Giving yourself permission to make a crooked yellow cup today opens the door to real growth. Start with what you have in your hands right now.

The English Class Excuse

That Monday at work, Daniel saw a bright poster on the office kitchen wall. A local library nearby hosted a free English conversation group every Thursday evening. Daniel stood holding his mug and read the poster three times. He wanted to speak English more smoothly in meetings, and this group was free and close to home. Yet a familiar voice inside his head told him to wait. First, he thought, he should review all his grammar notes and learn two hundred new words. Otherwise, he might freeze in front of strangers and feel embarrassed. Daniel walked back to his desk and told himself he was going to join next month instead. By Thursday evening, he stayed alone in his apartment, opened no grammar book, and felt disappointed in himself. He sat on his sofa looking at his phone while the conversation group met without him.

Maybe Daniel's excuse sounds familiar to you as an English learner. Many adults say, I will practice speaking after I finish my grammar book or learn more vocabulary. In real life, waiting for perfect English means you never open your mouth to speak. Speaking is a practical skill, much like riding a bicycle or swimming in a pool. You can read books about swimming for a whole year, yet you only learn inside the water. Mistakes in a conversation are not a sign that you started too early. They are simply proof that you are finally using the language in the real world. If you wait until you never make mistakes, you will wait forever. Try saying two short sentences out loud today, even if they are not smooth yet. Every short sentence you try trains your mouth and builds real courage.

Too Many Small Choices

On Tuesday night, Daniel decided to study English at home for one hour. He sat at his kitchen table with his laptop open and a fresh glass of water. Before starting, he searched online for the best English listening app. Ten minutes later, he had twelve open tabs on his screen. He read reviews, compared prices, and watched two long videos about study methods. Each time he liked one app, he worried that another app might be even better. Soon, the clock on his wall showed ten thirty at night. Daniel closed his laptop with tired eyes and rubbed his forehead. He spent ninety minutes looking at tools, and he practiced zero minutes of real English. Once again, his big study evening ended before it even started. He went to bed feeling busy in his mind, yet nothing had changed.

Planning feels safe because nobody can fail while they are still choosing an app. Searching for the best method tricks your mind into thinking you are already working hard. However, comparing ten different tools and making many small choices can feel heavy. After a long day of work, those extra decisions make you feel tired before the real task even begins. You do not need the top app or the best study plan on the internet. An ordinary tool that you actually open today helps you far more than a brilliant tool you only research. Think about how much time you spend choosing videos, books, or podcasts. Pick one simple resource that feels friendly enough right now. Close the other tabs, set a timer for ten minutes, and start with that single choice. Progress comes from doing, not from endless choosing.

The Missed Trip Sign-Up

Two weeks later, Hannah invited Daniel and three other friends on a weekend mountain hike. She sent a group message saying the bus seats filled up fast and closed on Friday at noon. Daniel read the message on Wednesday morning and smiled at the photos of green hills. He wanted to go, yet he hesitated before typing his answer. He wanted to check the weather report, buy better socks, and finish all his office work first. On Thursday, he got busy and forgot to reply to Hannah. On Friday afternoon, he opened his phone and checked the group chat. Hannah sent a cheerful message saying all the bus seats were full. Daniel stared at his screen in the quiet office hallway. For the first time, his habit of waiting caused clear regret. He missed a real weekend with friends because he could not decide on time.

That quiet moment in the hallway woke Daniel up in a way no advice book ever did. Sometimes, waiting costs us more than just a few wasted hours. When we put off decisions, real doors close while we are still standing outside. A small moment of regret can feel painful, yet it also teaches us something honest. Waiting for zero risk and perfect information means life moves on without us. Have you ever missed a trip, a class, or a good conversation because you waited too long to say yes? Remember that saying yes early does not mean you have every detail solved. You can figure out your socks or your schedule after you claim your seat. Next time a good chance appears, try answering before you feel one hundred percent sure. A fast, imperfect yes often brings better memories than a careful no.

Five Minutes After Dinner

On Sunday evening, Daniel washed his dinner dishes slowly. He looked up at the blue notebook on the shelf. This time, he did not promise a whole free hour. He did not promise a brand-new life on Monday either. Instead, he pulled the clean notebook down from the shelf. He told himself he only needed five short minutes. After five minutes, he was allowed to stop completely. Daniel sat down and opened the first blank page. His shoulders dropped, and he began writing right away. His handwriting looked messy, and his ideas felt very simple. Still, he kept writing for five full minutes. He listed ten minutes of walking and five English sentences. When the kitchen timer rang, Daniel closed the book softly. He smiled because the shelf was silent no longer. The notebook was finally open and alive.

Notice how different that evening was for Daniel. Earlier, he waited for a whole free hour and never touched the book. When he lowered the goal to five tiny minutes, his fear disappeared. In everyday life, starting is often the hardest part of any task. Once you sit down and write one line, continuing feels much easier than staring at a blank page. You can use this five-minute rule whenever a job feels too big or too heavy. Tell yourself you only have to read one page, wash five dishes, or listen to five minutes of English. If you want to stop after five minutes, you are completely allowed to stop. Five small minutes today always beat sixty imaginary minutes next week. What task can you shrink to five minutes tonight?

A Messy First Try

The following Thursday, Daniel walked into the local library fifteen minutes early for the English group. His hands felt slightly cold as he sat in a circle of eight friendly adults. When the leader asked everyone to share their week, Daniel's heart beat fast. During his turn, he forgot two simple past words and stopped twice to think. His face felt warm, and he wished his sentences sounded better. However, nobody laughed at him, and a woman across the circle looked over kindly and smiled. She shared her own story with gentle mistakes too, and everyone understood her clearly. After one hour, Daniel walked out into the cool night air feeling surprisingly proud. His English was far from perfect, yet he had finally spoken in a real group. He checked his watch and smiled because he had arrived on time and stayed until the end.

Why was Daniel proud even though he forgot two past words? Because real learning only happens when you step out of your safe plan and try. You can't learn to have natural conversations by staying silent in your apartment. When you speak a second language, mistakes are not failures or signs of wasted time. They show you exactly which words you need to practice tomorrow. In fact, listeners in real life care much more about your message and warmth than your grammar. Nobody expects a learner to walk into a first group speaking without stopping to think. Next time you want to wait until your English sounds smooth, remember Daniel in that library circle. Let yourself speak slowly, stop when you need to, and finish your thought anyway. Every messy conversation makes the next one feel a little calmer and easier.

Small Changes Over Weeks

One month later, Daniel opened his blue notebook again. Rain fell softly outside on that Sunday afternoon. Half of the pages now held short notes. Checked boxes and messy English words filled the lines. His life had not turned into a movie. He still missed a morning walk on busy days. Some evenings, he felt very tired after work. On those nights, he listened to English for five minutes. Still, four weeks brought a quiet, real change. He had joined three library groups in one month. He also walked fifteen mornings and finished two short stories. Daniel did not need huge energy or a perfect week. He simply showed up whenever he had a few minutes. He closed the book with calm, honest pride. A small coffee stain marked the blue cover now. Daniel liked his notebook much better that way.

Notice that Daniel's life did not change overnight in some magical way. He still felt tired on some evenings, and he still skipped days sometimes. Honest habits in adult life are never perfect straight lines. Progress looks more like a gentle path that goes up slowly over weeks and months. Missing one day does not ruin your effort, and it does not mean you failed. When you miss a day, you simply start again the next day without blaming yourself. Many learners quit because they think one missed evening takes away everything. In truth, small regular steps add up over time, even when each day looks ordinary. Do not look for dramatic changes in seven days; instead, look at what four quiet weeks of small starts can build. Slow growth is still real growth, and it lasts much longer.

When Later Comes Back

Today, Daniel still keeps that blue notebook right beside his morning coffee cup. Even now, on busy mornings or cold evenings, the quiet urge to wait still visits him. Sometimes he wants to put off a phone call, a workout, or a hard work email. When that happens, Daniel does not argue with himself for twenty minutes. Instead, he asks himself one simple question out loud. He asks, what is the smallest version of this task I can do in two minutes? Then he opens the email, writes the first greeting, or puts on his walking shoes. Yesterday, he met Hannah for lunch, and they finally signed up early for the next mountain hike. Daniel smiled as he clicked confirm, knowing the perfect moment never comes on its own. You only find the right moment by taking the first step.

Daniel is not a superhero who never hesitates again. He is an ordinary adult who learned one small skill. He knows how to act even when he feels unsure. You will still hear the word later this year. That quiet voice visits almost everyone at times. You do not have to fight it with big speeches. Instead, answer that voice with one gentle, simple question. Ask yourself what small piece you can start in two minutes. Making a task smaller turns a mountain into one easy step. That step sits right in front of your feet. Waiting for perfection brings more regret and wasted days. Starting small today brings real lessons and real confidence. When you feel stuck this week, smile and breathe slowly. Then begin with just two quiet minutes.
