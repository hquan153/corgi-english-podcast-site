Put Your Phone Down! Take Back Your Time! | Corgi English Podcast

Do you lose hours on your screen every day? Learn simple ways to put your phone down and enjoy real life again with calm English listening practice.

Today's goal

After this episode, you can talk about phone habits, use six useful words and four expressions, and try daily actions to spend less time on your screen.

From the creator

I have a bad habit when I have free time. Even if I only have one or two minutes, I pick up my phone and scroll. This feels meaningless and uses up a lot of time when it happens many times a day. My tip is to find one thing to learn or practice. Use your free time for those activities to grow as a person and make life more meaningful.

Key words

  • scroll: to move pictures or text up and down on a phone screen with your finger
  • notification: a short sound, light, or message from your phone that asks for your attention
  • screen time: the total number of minutes or hours you spend looking at a phone screen
  • habit: something you do often and almost without thinking about it every single day
  • focus: to give all of your attention to one thing without looking at anything else
  • on purpose: when you choose to do something with a clear plan and a clear reason

Useful expressions

  • “lose track of time”: to forget what time it is because you are busy with something
  • “take a break”: to stop what you are doing and rest for a short time
  • “in the moment”: giving your attention to what is happening right now around you
  • “call it a day”: to decide to stop working or looking at something for the rest of the day

Words for Better Screen Habits

Scroll means to move text or pictures up and down on a screen. You do this with your finger. Notification means a short sound or message from your phone. It asks for your attention right away. Screen time means the total hours you spend looking at your phone. Habit means something you do often without thinking about it. Focus means to give all your attention to one thing. The Five Minute Phone Trap

Emma: Now let us talk about a situation almost everyone knows well. Think about Daniel, a tired office worker who gets home at six in the evening. He sits on his sofa and says to himself, I will only check my phone for five minutes. Then he reads one message, watches one short clip, and sees another link. Suddenly, he looks at the clock and realizes fifty minutes have passed. He feels annoyed and still tired because he did not really rest or cook dinner. Those tiny five-minute checks steal big pieces of our evening without any warning. That small habit quietly takes away time we wanted for real life. Why does this happen so easily? Ryan, why do a few quick minutes on our phone turn into a whole lost hour?

Ryan: Small checks grow into big hours. Daniel is not lazy at all. Our brains love quick rewards, and phones give us tiny surprises every few seconds. When you scroll, you do not have a clear stopping point like the end of a book chapter. Each swipe brings a new picture or joke, so your brain keeps waiting for the next good one. Also, five minutes here and five minutes there add up very fast across one day. If you pick up your screen twelve times for five minutes, that is already one full hour of screen time. A simple trick can help right away. Before you unlock your phone, ask yourself what you want to do, and set a three-minute timer.

Why Phones Keep Us Watching

Emma: That timer idea makes so much sense, Ryan. Still, many people wonder why stopping feels so hard in the first place. Maybe you open one app after dinner to look at one photo from a friend. Ten minutes later, you are watching videos from strangers you do not even know. You feel a little frustrated because you wanted to go to bed early tonight. It feels like the screen has glue on it. You tell yourself to put it down, yet your thumb keeps moving. Of course, our phones do not actually control us, yet they feel very sticky. Why is it so hard to close an app once we open it? Ryan, what is happening inside these apps that keeps our eyes on the screen?

Ryan: Apps want your attention for as long as possible. Companies build these apps to keep us watching, not to help us finish quickly. Notice how the next video starts playing by itself without asking you. Notice how bright red dots and loud sounds pull your eyes back whenever a notification arrives. Every time you see something funny or surprising, your brain releases a feel-good chemical that makes you want a little more. That is why leaving the app feels slightly uncomfortable at first. When you understand this design, you can stop blaming yourself. Turn off auto-play in your video apps right away. You can also change your screen to black and white at night so colorful icons look less exciting. Your eyes will feel calmer, and putting the phone down becomes much easier.

Boredom Helps Your Brain Rest

Emma: Changing the screen colors is such a clever trick. Now let us think about what happens when we actually put the phone away. Today, many adults feel nervous when they have five quiet minutes with nothing to do. Waiting in line at the store or sitting at a bus stop feels empty without a screen. So we pull out our phones right away when we start to feel bored. Yet constant noise and fast pictures can make our minds feel very tired by the end of the day. We rarely give our thoughts any quiet space to slow down and breathe. Maybe being bored for a few minutes is actually good for us. Ryan, how can feeling bored for a few minutes help our brains and our mood?

Ryan: Quiet moments give your mind room to breathe. Think of Hannah, who takes a twenty-minute train ride to work every morning. Usually, she checks news and emails until she feels stressed before nine in the morning. One Tuesday, she decided to leave her phone inside her bag and just watch the trees pass the window. At first, she thought, this feels strange and slow. After five minutes, her shoulders dropped, and she suddenly remembered a great birthday gift idea for her mother. She arrived at her office feeling calmer and ready to focus. When your brain is not busy reading new things, it puts your memories in order and finds fresh ideas. Try waiting in line today without pulling out your phone, and notice how peaceful five quiet minutes can feel.

Using English Without Endless Scrolling

Emma: I love that story about Hannah. Feeling calm on a morning train sounds wonderful. Now, let us connect this topic to learning English. Many learners use their phones to study English every day, and that makes total sense. You have podcasts, word lists, and study cards right in your pocket. However, you probably know how fast study time can turn into play time. Maybe you open your phone to watch one short English lesson. Twenty minutes later, you are watching cooking clips or funny cats instead. You close the screen feeling guilty because you barely practiced English at all. Your brain learns best when you stay calm and focused. Ryan, how can English learners use technology on purpose without falling into endless scrolling?

Ryan: Study on purpose, then put the screen away. You do not need to throw away your phone to learn well. Instead, create clear borders around your English practice. First, download an audio lesson or podcast before you leave home, and turn off your internet while you listen. Second, keep a small paper notebook and a pen next to you for writing new words by hand. Writing on paper helps your memory and stops you from jumping between ten apps. Third, choose one goal before you start, such as reading one short story or reviewing ten words. When you finish that small goal, close your phone and step away. This simple habit protects your focus and helps your English grow steadily.

Good Teacher or Time Thief

She does not have space in her bag for heavy textbooks or paper word books. On her phone, she opens one audio lesson and checks three new words in seconds. Because her phone is always with her, she practices English during moments that were completely empty before. When you use learning apps on purpose, your screen becomes a portable classroom that saves you hours every week. That kind of easy access helps learners stay consistent.

Ryan: I see your point about Sofia, Emma, yet I see things differently. For many people, a phone acts more like a thief of time than a helpful guide. Think about Ben, a young designer who wants to review English grammar after dinner. He opens his phone to check one verb rule. Emma: You make a good point, Ryan. Fun apps pull us away very easily. Still, the phone itself is not the real problem. It does not force us to open funny clips. Phones Outside the Bedroom

Small bedroom habits shape how rested you feel the next morning. So tell us what works in your home. Do you leave your phone in another room at night, or keep it nearby, and why?

She picks up her screen every ten minutes just to check. In that way, one good alert protects her peace of mind. It actually saves her from checking all day long.

Even when he does not pick up the phone, that tiny sound breaks his focus. After each ring, he needs several minutes to remember his last sentence. By five in the afternoon, he feels exhausted from starting over twenty times. For most of us, too many notifications act like a noisy trap that steals deep work.

Emma: You make a strong point about Omar and his report, Ryan. Store sales and random news alerts definitely interrupt our focus and waste our energy. Daily Steps Away From Screens

Third, after work, put your phone on a shelf for thirty minutes while you walk or talk with someone. Finally, one hour before bed, plug your phone into a charger outside your bedroom. You do not need to change everything at once. Even doing one of these actions tomorrow will give you back real time. Ryan, how can listeners keep doing these steps day after day?

Ryan: Make the new habit easy to win. Starting small is the best way to stick with any daily change. Begin with just one quiet morning rule, like waiting fifteen minutes before touching your screen, and celebrate each small success. Next, prepare the night before by leaving your charger in the hall and putting a paper book beside your bed. On Sunday evening, spend two minutes checking your weekly screen time number so you can track your steady progress. Watch how that number drops week by week. You can also connect this habit to your English practice. Take fifteen screen-free minutes each evening to read three pages of an English story or speak aloud about your day. Small steady steps build habits that really last.

Helpful Phrases and Daily Promises

Emma: Let us practice four natural English expressions today. Number one is lose track of time. It means to forget the time when you are busy. For example, Daniel scrolled after lunch and lost track of time. Number two is take a break. It means to stop your work and rest for a short time. For example, let us take a break and walk outside. Number three is in the moment. It means giving your attention to what is happening right now. For example, put your phone away so you stay in the moment. Number four is call it a day. It means to stop doing something for the rest of the day. For example, it is nine at night, so let us call it a day.

Ryan: Your words shape your daily choices. Try speaking these four helpful promises out loud with me right now, nice and slow. First, repeat this sentence clearly. I am in control of my time today. Saying that out loud reminds you that your hours belong to you. Next, try this one with confidence. I can put my phone down and enjoy real life. Picture a quiet walk or a warm cup of tea when you say it. Our third sentence is all about choice. I will use my phone on purpose, not just when I feel bored. Finally, say this last promise gently. I am present with the people around me. Repeating these calm lines in the morning helps you protect your focus and feel proud of your day.
