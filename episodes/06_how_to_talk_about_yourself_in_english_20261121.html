Key words

  • introduce: To tell someone your name and a few simple facts about your life.
  • background: Your family, hometown, school, and past work that make you who you are.
  • hobby: An activity that you enjoy doing in your free time for fun.
  • goal: Something important that you want to do or reach in the future.
  • detail: One small, clear fact or feeling that makes a story more interesting.
  • follow-up question: A short question you ask someone to keep a friendly talk going.

Useful expressions

  • “break the ice”: To say something friendly to start a conversation and help people feel relaxed.
  • “in my free time”: During the hours when you are not working or studying.
  • “used to”: Something you did often in the past, or something that was true before, but not now.
  • “these days”: Right now in your current life, during this period of time.

Common mistakes

  • Many people list their name, age, job, and hometown like a robot without any feeling. → Pick two or three facts and add one small detail about why you like them.
  • Many learners say "I am work in an office" or "I have twenty-five years old" because they translate directly. → Say "I work in an office" and "I am twenty-five", and practice these two sentences until they feel automatic.
  • People often stop speaking after their answer and forget to ask the other person a question. → Always finish with a simple follow-up question like "How about you" or "What do you do".

Words to Share Your Life

Why Talking Feels Hard

Emma: Let's start with a feeling that almost every learner knows well. Think of Maya. She is at a work dinner with new people from another city. Someone smiles and asks, "So, what do you do?" Why does our brain freeze like this when we just want to talk about our own life? Ryan, what is happening in our heads during those quiet seconds?

Ryan: Too many jobs at once. That is the real problem. When you translate in your head, your brain is doing two hard tasks at the exact same moment. First, you are searching for facts about your life. Second, you are building English grammar from zero. That heavy load makes you feel tired and stressed fast. When you feel stressed, it's even harder to remember simple words you already know. The good news is that your personal story does not change every day. You have the same hometown, the same job, and the same favorite hobby this week. If you prepare three or four ready sentences in English before you go out, your brain can relax. You don't need to translate from zero anymore.

The Ten-Second Hello

Emma: That makes so much sense, Ryan. Preparing a few ready sentences takes the pressure right off. Now, maybe you are walking into a new English class or joining a video call for the first time. You don't want to give a long speech about your whole life. People are busy, and you just want to introduce yourself in a warm, natural way. A ten-second hello is perfect for these quick moments. You just need your name and one simple fact about what you do or where you are from. Even a short greeting can make a strong, friendly start. Still, many learners wonder which fact to pick first. Ryan, what does a great ten-second hello sound like in real life?

Ryan: Short and warm. That is all you need for the first ten seconds. Think about how people actually greet each other at a coffee shop or an online meeting. Nobody expects your whole life story right away. You can use a simple pattern: your name, plus where you are from or what you do. For example, "Hi, I'm Lucy, and I'm from a small town by the sea. I work in a book shop." That takes less than ten seconds, and it's completely clear. If you are a student, you can say, "Hello, I'm Carlos, and I'm studying nursing." Keep your voice calm and smile a little when you speak. People will remember your warmth much more than your grammar. A quick, friendly hello opens the door for a nice chat.

Adding One Small Detail

Emma: I love how simple that pattern is. Now, what happens when you have a little more time to talk? Think of Leo. He joined a weekend walking group in his town. When a friendly woman asked about his job, Leo said, "I'm an accountant. I live in the north part of town. I have one cat." He gave three facts, and then the talk stopped. Both of them stood there looking at their shoes. Leo felt a little awkward because he wanted to keep chatting. He realized that a list of facts feels cold, like reading a form. One tiny change can make a big difference. Ryan, how can we turn a dry list of facts into something people actually enjoy hearing?

Ryan: One small detail changes everything. Facts tell people who you are, but a detail helps them feel what you are like. Instead of stopping at "I'm an accountant," Leo can add one short feeling or reason. He can say, "I'm an accountant, and I really love solving number puzzles." Or about his cat, he can say, "I have one old cat who sleeps on my keyboard every morning." Suddenly, the other person smiles and has something easy to talk about. You don't need long, hard words to add a detail. Just ask yourself, "Why do I like this?" or "What is one funny thing about this?" That one extra sentence turns a boring list into a real human moment.

Talking About Your Hobby

Emma: A cat on a keyboard is such a fun detail! People can picture that right away. Another great way to share who you are is to talk about a hobby. When you talk about something you truly enjoy, your voice naturally sounds happier and more relaxed. Maybe you like baking bread, running in the park, or watching old movies on Friday nights. You don't need to be an expert at your hobby to talk about it. Even if you only do it once a month, it's still part of your life. Many learners get stuck on the grammar when they describe their free time. Ryan, what are the easiest sentences to use when we talk about a hobby?

Ryan: Simple present sentences are your best friend here. You can start with "I like," "I enjoy," or "I love." Remember that after these three words, we usually use the "-ing" form of the action word. For example, say "I enjoy gardening on Saturday mornings," or "I love cooking spicy noodles for my friends." Then, add one quick reason why you enjoy it. You can say, "It helps me relax after a busy week at the office." Or you can say, "I'm into photography because I like noticing small things outside." Notice how clear and natural those sentences sound. When you talk about your hobby with simple present words, you sound confident and easy to understand. Pick one hobby tonight and practice two sentences about it.

Connecting English to Life

Emma: Practicing sentences about your own hobby is such a smart way to learn. In fact, talking about yourself is one of the best tools for improving your English overall. Think about why. When you learn words from a textbook about topics you never use, you forget them quickly. But when you learn words to describe your own job, your goal, and your favorite food, you care about those words. You will use them again and again in real life. Every time you share your story, you practice speaking without feeling like you are taking a test. It turns English into something personal and useful. Ryan, why does practicing our own story help us feel calmer and learn faster at the same time?

Ryan: Personal words stick in your memory much better. Your brain remembers things connected to your real life. It also remembers things linked to your real feelings. Practice talking about your own goal or background. You don't have to invent a fake story. You already know the true facts about yourself. So you can focus on the English sounds. Talking about yourself starts almost every friendly conversation. When you feel calm at first, your shoulders drop. Your breathing also gets slow and easy. That calm feeling stays for the rest of the talk. Learn ten or fifteen sentences about your own life. That builds a strong base for every future conversation. Small practice on your own story brings real confidence.

Keeping the Talk Going

Emma: Building that strong base for the first minute really changes the whole mood of a chat. Now, let's think about what happens right after you finish your short introduction. Think of Sara. She practiced her three sentences about her job and her weekend hobby. When a new neighbor asked about her life, Sara spoke clearly and smiled. She felt proud! She forgot one important piece at the end of her turn. Ryan, how can we pass the ball to the other person so the talk keeps going?

Ryan: End with a question. A good conversation is like a game of catch, not a solo speech. When you finish sharing your detail, toss the ball back with a short follow-up question. It shows that you care about the other person, too. Best of all, it gives you a nice break to listen and breathe! You only need two or three easy questions in your pocket. You can say, "How about you?" or "What do you do in your free time?" If someone tells you their job, you can ask, "Do you enjoy it?" People love talking about their own lives. One simple follow-up question keeps the chat warm, balanced, and easy for both of you. You will be surprised how well this works.

Listing Too Many Facts

Emma: Now let's look at three common mistakes people make when they talk about themselves, and how to fix them easily. The first mistake is listing too many facts at once. Think of Sam. He wanted to give a good answer in his English class. So he wrote down eight facts and tried to remember every one. When the teacher called on him, Sam spoke fast. He listed his name, age, hometown, job, brothers, sisters, hobbies, and favorite color without stopping. He did that because he thought more facts meant better English. Instead, he sounded like a robot, and his classmates could not remember anything he said. Ryan, why is it better to share less and add a small detail instead?

Ryan: Less is more when you introduce yourself. Our brains can only hold a few new pieces of information at one time. When you give a long list of eight facts, the listener does not remember most of them right away. Also, talking like a list of facts leaves no room for your personality. Instead, pick just two or three facts that matter most in that moment. Then add one small detail about why you like one of them. For example, say, "I'm Sam, and I'm from a busy city in the north. I'm a chef, and I love making fresh bread because the smell makes people happy." That is warm, human, and much easier for you to say. People will remember you with a smile.

Translating Word for Word

Emma: The second mistake is one that almost every English learner makes at some point, and it's completely normal. It happens when we translate word for word from our first language. Think of Ana. She was chatting with a new person at work and wanted to sound polite and clear. Because her native language uses the verb "have" for age, she quickly translated it in her head. She also added an extra "am" before her action verb. It is so easy to slip into those habits when you are nervous and speaking fast. You know the real grammar rule, but the old habit wins in the moment. Ryan, how can we fix these two common slips and make the right sentences feel natural?

Ryan: Start with the exact correct pattern. Many learners say, "I am work in an office," or "I have twenty-five years old." Those slips happen because of direct translation from another language. In English, we do not need "am" before "work," and we use "am" for age, not "have." Listen closely to the right way. You say, "I work in an office." Again, "I work in an office." And for age, you say, "I am twenty-five." Once more, "I am twenty-five." When you practice these two short sentences out loud ten times at home, your mouth remembers the right shape. Then, in a real chat, the correct English comes out automatically without thinking. Simple, repeated practice beats direct translation every single time.

Forgetting to Ask Back

Emma: That practice tip is going to save so many people from those little slips. Now let's talk about our third common mistake, which is forgetting to ask a question back. Think of Ken. He met a friendly traveler on a train. The traveler asked Ken where he was from and what he liked to do on weekends. Ken gave a great answer with a nice detail about hiking in the mountains. Then Ken smiled, looked out the window, and stopped talking. He was not trying to be rude at all. He was just so relieved that he finished his English sentences! Still, the traveler felt like Ken did not want to talk. Ryan, how can we remember to pass the conversation back?

Ryan: Treat your question as part of your answer. When you practice your introduction at home, never stop after your own detail. Always glue one follow-up question to the very end of your sentence. That way, your answer does not feel finished until you ask back. You can use two super easy questions that work almost everywhere. You can say, "How about you?" or "What do you do?" For example, "I love hiking in the mountains on Sundays. How about you?" Notice how natural that sounds. It takes only three extra words, and it shows warm interest in the other person. Once you make asking back a habit, every conversation will feel much easier and warmer. People will always be happy to talk with you.

Friendly Phrases and Daily Confidence

Emma: Now let's learn four useful English expressions that fit naturally when you talk about yourself. I will explain each one slowly with a clear example. Our first expression is break the ice, which means to say something friendly to start a conversation and help people feel relaxed. For example, "Asking about the weather is an easy way to break the ice." Next, in my free time means during the hours when you are not working or studying. For example, "In my free time, I like to bake bread." Another useful phrase is used to. This means something you did often in the past, or something that was true before, but not now. For example, "I used to live in a small town." Finally, these days means right now in your current life. For example, "These days, I am learning to play the guitar."
